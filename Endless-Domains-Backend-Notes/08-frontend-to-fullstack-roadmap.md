# 08. From Frontend to Fullstack, Using This Codebase as the Map

## What you actually just did

You now have notes on eighty one files' worth of analysis covering a real, live, several years old production backend, written by reading its actual code, not a summary of it. That is not a small thing, and it is worth naming directly, most people trying to move from frontend into fullstack work spend their time on tutorials and small toy projects, and never once practice the specific skill of opening a codebase this size that nobody explained to them and building an accurate mental model of it. You just did that, across eleven separate business domains. This closing note is about turning that into a concrete plan for what to actually learn next, using this exact codebase, and this exact company's business, as the map instead of a generic list.

## The skill ladder, anchored to what you just read

Start with what you already have, since frontend experience is a real, transferable head start, not a deficit to overcome. You already understand HTTP requests and responses, because you have made countless `fetch` or `axios` calls. You already understand forms and validation, because you have built countless UIs that reject bad input before submitting it. You already understand authentication from the client side, storing a token, attaching it to a request, redirecting after login. Everything in the auth cluster's notes ([clusters/01-auth-and-identity](clusters/01-auth-and-identity/00-README.md)), and specifically its closing bridge note, is written to connect that existing knowledge directly to what happens on the other side of those exact same actions, hashing a password, signing a JWT, verifying a signature. Read that note again once you have read this whole roadmap, it will land differently the second time.

The next rung is data modeling and relationships, the actual subject of your earlier LearnBridge planning work at `LearnBridge-Capstone/planning`. Every relationship shape you planned there, one to many, many to many with its own attached data, one to one, actually exists in this real codebase too, an instructor to their courses is the same shape as a user to their owned domains, an enrollment carrying its own progress data is the same shape as this app's `Enrollment` adjacent entities in the loyalty and commerce clusters carrying their own state. The difference between a planned, clean project and this real one is exactly the difference worth studying, LearnBridge has one correct way to do each thing, this real codebase has the same idea implemented three or four slightly different ways by three or four different people over several years, and reconciling those differences in your head is the actual job.

The next rung is the one most frontend developers have never touched at all, infrastructure and configuration. Read [06-infrastructure-and-deployment.md](06-infrastructure-and-deployment.md) and [03-configuration-and-secrets.md](03-configuration-and-secrets.md) again, slowly, and then go find your own current employer's or your own side project's equivalent of a Terraform file, a deployment script, or a secrets manager, even if it is much smaller. The specific tools (Terraform, AWS Secrets Manager, ECS) matter less than the concept, a real application's configuration and its actual running infrastructure are not the same thing as its source code, and a fullstack engineer is expected to move comfortably between all three, not just the third one.

The last rung, and the one this whole exercise was secretly training the whole time, is reading unfamiliar code critically rather than passively. [07-security-and-quality-findings-index.md](07-security-and-quality-findings-index.md) is not a list of things to feel bad about on this company's behalf, it is a worked example of exactly the kind of thing a senior backend or fullstack engineer notices automatically after enough repetition, one code path doing something a very similar path does not, a piece of dead code nobody removed, a secret that should never have been committed. That instinct is trainable, and the way to train it is exactly what you just watched happen eleven times in parallel, read real code, compare similar things against each other, and say out loud, specifically, what is different and whether that difference is deliberate or accidental.

## A concrete next project, using what you now know about this codebase

Do not try to contribute to this exact codebase as your first step unless your job already gives you a supervised, low risk way in, it is too large and too consequential to learn on directly. Instead, go back to `LearnBridge-Capstone/planning` and, before writing any code there, add one deliberate new requirement to its PRD, taken directly from a real pattern you just read about here, for instance a many to many relationship where both sides need to stay in sync (the way `Developer` and `Project` did in your MongoDB notes, and the way `affilate` and `affiliate-user` accidentally ended up doing here), or a webhook receiver that must verify a real signature using a raw request body the way [clusters/09-events-and-webhooks/02-signature-verification-deep-dive.md](clusters/09-events-and-webhooks/02-signature-verification-deep-dive.md) describes, built correctly this time, with a test that proves a wrong signature actually gets rejected. Building the correct version of something you just watched go wrong in a real system is a far stronger exercise than building it in a vacuum.

## The honest, final test

You are ready to call yourself meaningfully progressed toward fullstack, not finished, nobody is ever finished, when you can pick any single feature folder in this real codebase you have not yet had summarized for you, open it cold, and produce your own short version of what one of these twelve cluster notes did, in your own words, checked against the real code, before reading whatever an AI or a teammate has already written about it. That is the actual skill every note in this entire folder was quietly modeling for you the whole time.

## Update from the October 2026 uat pull

The new [cluster 12](clusters/12-marketplace-v2/00-README.md) gives you three better exercises than anything the older code offered, because for the first time the patterns are mostly built well, with tests, so you can copy their good parts and avoid their documented mistakes.

The first is a validated config loader. In your own project, replace any scattered `process.env.SOMETHING` reads with one loader that reads every key once, validates each with a small function that names the bad key in its error, and hands the rest of the app a typed object, the way `src/components/marketplacev2/config/chain-config.loader.ts` does. Then add the thing this codebase forgot: a test that fails if your `.env.sample` lists a key name the loader does not read.

The second is cursor pagination. Add a cursor based list endpoint, modelled on `order/util/order-cursor.util.ts`, with a stable tie breaker (sort by the value, then by id). Then deliberately fix the two problems note 06 found: reject a cursor whose sort key does not match the requested sort with a 400, and store your timestamps as `timestamptz`. Build the React infinite scroll that consumes it.

The third is a small background worker that is safe to run twice. Write a job that processes rows in order and keeps a cursor, then start two copies of your app at once and watch it break. Fix it with a Postgres advisory lock or a conditional `UPDATE ... WHERE last = :expected`, and write a test for the case where two copies run together. That one exercise teaches more about real backend work than almost anything else in this folder, and it is the exact problem [clusters/12-marketplace-v2/08-the-onchain-event-poller-architecture.md](clusters/12-marketplace-v2/08-the-onchain-event-poller-architecture.md) describes.

One more habit this update rewards: open a feature's `.spec.ts` before its implementation. Marketplace v2's test titles are often the clearest statement of intent in the code, and checking whether each test really asserts what its title claims (several do not, see the update section of [07-security-and-quality-findings-index.md](07-security-and-quality-findings-index.md)) is good practice for reviewing other people's pull requests.
